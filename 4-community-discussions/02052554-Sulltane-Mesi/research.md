# Disscusions in the Software Engineering community about AI

## ABSTRACT
This paper summarizes some of the ongoing discussions among software engineers regarding AI-supported development and whether it is replacing developers or transforming the roles and expectations of the profession for the future with evidence from labor markets, debates about productivity, technical debt, and software quality, and how many developers view AI as an amplifier: good engineering practices will make AI helpful, and poor practices will create more technical debt and lower-quality code. It also dives in the more objective parts of the profession such as technical debt and internship decline within the last few years. It creates an interesting contrast between facts and personal experiences of software engineers that have taken to blogs, forms and social media to share their pro and against opinions against AI. This paper goes through all phases such as introduction of the topic to the main body which lists different sources that made this paper  be as informative as it could be and then explains the methodology to then double down on the prior discussions. All in all, it shows that this whole situation is a coin with two sides where each of them have their benefits and their disadvantages when it comes to different spheres of the engineering community. 

## INTRODUCTION
As AI continues to conquer every space of life that we live in at a rapid pace, it makes it harder to ignore what tomorrow might bring not only for the field but for all of society. Due to this, more and more businesses, governments, healthcare systems,  and individuals rely more and more on it and because of that the responsibilities of software engineers expand in a field that has become one of the most dynamic engineering disciplines in the modern technological landscape.
While traditional software engineering focused more on structured programming, debugging and system maintenance, current technological innovation has changed the game forever. Newer technologies such as AI-assisted development and cloud native application architectures are fundamentally changing the what that software is designed, developed, tested, deployed and maintained. AI’s relevance to software development is significant due to its ability to learn patterns and most importantly implement them automatically when needed. This paper argues that AI is not simply replacing software engineers but it is changing the meaning of what it is that engineers do and what they love doing. 

## LITERATURE REVIEW
In the literature and community discussions about AI in software engineering, it is clear that AI-assisted development is not a small or experimental topic anymore, but rather a central debate in the software engineering community, as developers, researchers, and companies ask questions such as, Will AI replace programmers, will AI change the role of junior developers, and can I trust AI-generated code in professional software systems? As AI is increasingly used to write, review, and deploy code, developers are spending more time specifying requirements, prompting AI tools, integrating generated code, validating outputs, and making higher-level engineering decisions, a pattern of activity that is consistent with the research of Tom Zimmermann on the future of the developer profession.

## SOFTWARE ENGINEERING COMMUNITY DISCUSSIONS
The software engineering community is divided on how they feel about the usage of AI within the field. While some developers see AI as a natural evolution of software engineering and programming that is following a similar path as compilers, IDEs and auto complete while others are concerned about losing their jobs, skills and their code ownership to AI.Within the software engineering community, most discussions focus less on the complete replacement of software engineers and more on trust, junior opportunities, technical debt and most importantly, the long-term quality of software systems. The same type of discussions and concerns are talked about on different community spaces, such as: Reddit, engineering blogs, podcasts and surveys. Most asked questions involve such: Will there still be a place for junior developers? Can we trust AI-generated code? Are productivity gains real? Are teams creating more technical debt than they can handle?


## DEVELOPER’S FUTURE AND TIME MANAGEMENT
One big theme in software engineering was developer identity, work and the future of software engineering. Many opinions within the community seem to be incompatible seeing that while some believe that coding is dead, others consider AI as a step to evolution. Based on prof. Tom Zimmermann’s study on the future of developer profession, where he touches on what a developer is, what it means to be a developer today and how that meaning will shift with the popularization of AI, it is mentioned that if AI keeps being integrated in code writing, reviewing, and deployment, the developer role might have to change lanes towards specifying, prompting, integrating, validating and making higher-level decisions.
Some believe it is just a natural part of evolution while others  feel as is they’re losing the part of the job they enjoyed the most.
Moreover, Annie Vella’s research on developer’s new roles and tasks gives us insight on how software engineer’s time allocation has shifted across six core development tasks. According to the research writing code showed the strongest reduction, with 82% reporting less time. The only task engineers reported spending more time and was slightly above neutral is reviewing code.

## TRUSTING AI GENERATED CODE
In accordance with many programmers and engineers, code quality has fallen quite low. Whenever developers might use AI to simply debug the code, it will go ahead and rewrite formatting. For some, whenever they ask a LLM to write a piece of code for them, it creates pieces made up of syntax bugs that while aren’t hard to spot can represent issues if not for compilers emitting warnings by catching the indention. 
A user from Reddit named SwiftOneSpeaks suggests that while superficial code quality has gone up, maintainability and understanding the actual requirements have gone down. They believe that LLMs can auto complete tiresome tasks for existing developers but it’s destructive whenever critical thinking and actual problem solving skills are expected. 
Stack Overflow’s 2025 Developer Survey found that more developers distrust AI accuracy than trust it. This is important because it shows that AI tools are popular but still not fully reliable in the eyes of developers. However, the percentage of professional developers that believed that AI tools struggled with complex tasks has fallen from 35% to 29% in just over 2 years.
The reason for this discussion is quite simple; as mentioned above, code can look correct while being wrong in many cases. It may use the wrong library, produce insecure code or misunderstand the current code which beginners might not notice but as we talked about, experienced developers notice almost immediately. 
While they still may use AI to create the code, it still leaves a kind of a trust but verify kind of situation which is less than ideal for bigger projects assuming that it takes more time to check and debug the code by yourself while not being familiar with the layout and it might cost more than just having a go at it yourself. 

## LABOR MARKET EVIDENCE
The debate is underpinned by a set of labor market data that neither side is wholly comfortable with. First, the signals of disruption are real: Stanford economists associated with the Digital Economy Lab found that, over a three-year period ending in 2025, early-career workers in AI-exposed fields lost 13 percent of their jobs; new graduate hiring for software roles in 2023 was half what it was in 2019 (one of the swiftest job shifts in any career); research firm SignalFire reported that major technology companies cut new graduate hiring 25 percent in 2024 versus 2023; and by early 2026, adoption had grown so fast that AI-generated code accounted for nearly half of all code, far exceeding initial estimates. For coding boot-camps, placement rates were also much lower, as many of the tasks that junior developers were asked to do were automated by AI tools. However, aggregate demand for software engineering has not fallen off a cliff; According to an analysis by the Economic Policy Institute, employment for software engineers was 7 percent higher in 2025 than in 2019, and job openings for software engineers were 10 percent higher in 2025 than in 2019.
Moreover, another study by Samuel Westby and Alicia S. Modestino argues that generative AI is significantly reshaping the job market for software developers, particularly junior-level positions. Following the release of Chat-GPT in November 2022, there was a 16.3% decline in junior-level job vacancies compared to senior-level positions, despite controlling for various factors like geography and industry. This decline is most pronounced in larger firms and cities, while industries with lower exposure to AI technologies have fared better. The study also explores alternative career paths for junior developers potentially displaced by AI, suggesting policies such as apprenticeships could help maintain the talent pipeline.

## JUNIOR DEVELOPERS AND THE LOSS OF ENTRY-LEVEL LEARNING
A great blog  mentioning How AI has changed the career pathway for junior developers talks about how for Gen Z students, the promised career as a software engineer was their golden ticket to a stable future and success but due to AI that career promise is now gone. Pheobe Sajor, a CS major talks about her life in the late 2000s as a teen playing around with the internet which was rapidly evolving, “still rough around the edges” as she likes to call it. Back then, the curiosity of this new era took many kids by surprise. They had a whole new world to explore and based on statistics a safe world at that. As many took to Computer Science in university, it was quickly seen that it afforded them an early sense of career security, some even going as far as dating CS majors to give themselves a hope of security and safety for the future. 
Much of that shine has faded throughout the years. Perhaps, it has gone away entirely, especially after the pandemic where the job market proved itself volatile with job cuts reaching their highest midyear total since COVID as shown on the Newsweek article. Due to this, many workers belonging to GEN Z (64%) are worried about being laid off as compared to the 45% of the millennial counterparts. She writes that GEN Z is also the generation most concerned about a recession as though for people aged 22-27 the unemployment rate is nearly double the national average of 4.2% , sitting at 7.4% as of June 2025.
## GOODBYE INTERNS, HELLO CHATBOTS
Pheobe goes on to talk about how throughout the years students have had to land internship after internship to position themselves somewhere in the very competitive job market they had to live in. Even if those companies forgot about them after they are done with the internship, if it meant they got the credit for it, they’d do it.
In a 2024 survey of hiring managers found that 70% of them believed that AI could do the job of an intern and 57% of those surveyed said they trust AI’s work more than the work of interns or recent grads.
Studies show that year after year , internships have decrease 11% according to Indeed.

## PRODUCTIVITY DEBATES - IS AI ACCELERATING TECHNICAL DEBT?
Based on a productivity debate blog, the main discussion says that AI may be increasing technical debt at the moment, but not necessarily at a greater rate than before. The best yes argument is that AI generation is moving faster than software teams’ review, testing and maintenance can handle. Developers are creating more code and using AI more and more, however they seem worried that this speed will create hidden problems as they go. A developer named Brian adds that companies care more about speed rather than code cleanliness so, in his opinion, AI is not only increasing normal technical debt but also cognitive debt as though referring to developers understanding their own systems because AI writes more of their code than they themselves do. 

## METHODOLOGY
This paper uses a qualitative research approach by analyzing discussions from the software engineering community. The sources used, include developer surveys, engineering blogs, Reddit discussions and different research papers based on AI-assisted software development. Developer surveys and labor market research provide broader data while Reddit discussions and blogs reveal personal experiences straight from developers and programmers who experience AI tools in their daily work. The paper focuses on many discussions on different themes that these discussions talk about: the future role of developers, trust in AI-generated code,  the changing labor market for junior developers, productivity debates, and the risk of technical debt.
On the other hand, others like to argue that there is a difference between absolute and proportional technical debt. AI might create more technical debt as it helps teams produce more code but this doesn’t necessarily mean that the overall code is worse than before. If teams decide to use testing tools and agents to deal with maintenance, they might be able to control the damage. Still,  concerns remain because all this code created is faster than the teams that are willing to understand and maintain them.
## FINDINGS
In particular, ResultsFirst showed that the new role is redefining software engineers, not replacing them, with discussions reviewed in this paper indicating that developers spend more time reviewing, validating, and managing the output of AI than they do writing code, which can change the developer role from one that produces code to one that oversees and enhances code. Second, a major concern within the software engineering community is the future of the junior developer, who has historically learned the craft by executing simple coding tasks, fixing bugs, writing tests, and reading code, all tasks that AI can also perform, meaning that companies may restrict junior hires or expect new developers to be productive more rapidly.

## DISCUSSION
It is not just that AI is driving one fundamental change in software engineering, but multiple changes simultaneously. AI may be a productivity tool that frees up time for some developers so that they can work on more complex decisions, but for others it creates anxiety because the skills that made programming feel valuable are changing. Evidence in the labor market suggests that junior developers may be more affected by AI than senior developers, which fits the hypothesis that AI does not replace all programmers equally, but rather those tasks that are structured, repetitive, and at the beginner level. Senior developers remain advantaged as they know architecture, business requirements, debugging, and long-term system maintenance. The trust and technical debt discussions illustrate that AI is useful but hazardous. 

## CONCLUSION
While AI is not replacing software engineers, it is changing the way that the community works. Discussions among software engineering community show that developers are worried about trust, technical debt, productivity, junior developer opportunities, and software quality. AI can produce code rapidly, but speed does not equate to good software, which still requires review, testing, understanding, and maintenance. The most compelling point is that AI replaces tasks but not responsibility. In the future, programmers will write less code by hand, but they will have to be more adept at reviewing code, validating AI output, understanding systems, and making engineering decisions. This creates both danger and opportunity for junior developers, as AI can be used as a learning tool but can also become a crutch that weakens learning. Thus, the evolution of software engineering will not be solely determined by how powerful AI becomes but also by how responsibly developers, companies, and regulators use it.
